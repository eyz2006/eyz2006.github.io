**Project Personal Writeup**
Our project examines patterns of police misconduct in New York City using two public datasets from NYC Open Data. We focused on the three research questions: how extreme the highest-complaint officers are relative to the overall distribution, which officer groups show the highest complaint burden and substantiation intensity, and whether precincts with higher crime volume also tend to have more misconduct allegations. By combining officer-level data from the Civilian Complaint Review Board with precinct-level crime data from the NYPD, our goal is to provide a multi-level analysis that connects individual behavior, group patterns, and broader environmental context. This allows us to move beyond simple counts of complaints and instead explore how misconduct is distributed and whether it is concentrated in specific officers, groups, or locations.

Over the course of the project, our research questions became more focused and structured. Initially, we were more interested in patterns of police misconduct, but we refined this into three clearly defined analytical levels after finding our second dataset: officer-level distribution (RQ1), group-level risk patterns (RQ2), and precinct-level relationships between crime and misconduct (RQ3). We also evolved in our visualizations, such as a shift from using the Gini coefficient to show how uneven complaints were across groups to building a more comparable visualization, after assessing the results of our data. We then changed it to a quadrant bubble chart, which is more visual and lets us compare two things at once. Our evolution helped us to move from descriptive exploration to more targeted analysis. We also adjusted how we framed our third question from simply comparing crime and misconduct to more carefully examining whether there is an association between them—after recognizing that wording like “crime volume” could be misleading or overly causal. Overall, the project evolved from a general exploration into a more structured and policy-relevant investigation.

Our project is built around a structured data pipeline and an interactive Streamlit application. We used BigQuery to store and query large datasets efficiently, with separate workflows for officer-level data and precinct-level crime data. For Dataset 1, we clean and standardize variables such as command and complaint counts before using them for both individual and group-level analysis. For Dataset 2, we aggregate crime incidents by precinct and then merge them with misconduct data using aligned precinct identifiers. The Streamlit app is organized into pages corresponding to each research question, allowing users to explore distributions, group comparisons, and precinct-level patterns through visualizations. To improve performance and reliability, we implemented caching, modular helper functions, and careful data cleaning steps to ensure consistency across analyses. We also used pytest and Pandera to ensure we catch errors that are unobservable early, which helps our data to sustain its data chain and loop.

For my key learnings from a development perspective, one thing that I learnt was how much coding is connected to user experience and directly affects interpretation. For example, without adding summary metrics or correlation values, users might misinterpret patterns in the visualizations. This is closely tied to the data pipeline and we found debugging the pipeline is harder than debugging the interface, as errors in aggregation or merging don’t always produce visible failures. This relies heavily on our pytests and pandera, which also made us realize how important it is to enforce a system with validations. Another learning was when I realized how important code style and formatting is when we tried to get our code to pass Ruff. At first, it seemed minor, but even small issues like trailing whitespace or long lines caused the checks to fail. We realized that writing chunks of code under a pipeline with validation is not enough, as it is also important for us to maintain a clean, consistent, and readable code for this collaborative project to make sense.
